About the company

Storebrand dates its history to 1767, when a scheme for fire insurance of buildings in Norwegian towns was set up. The name comes from a fire insurer for goods and movable property founded in Christiania in 1847. The most dramatic period came after the merger with UNI Forsikring in 1991. The following year UNI Storebrand failed in its attempt to build a Nordic insurance group around Sweden's Skandia, the holding company was placed under public administration, and only after a refinancing in 1993 did the shares return to the stock exchange.

Today Storebrand is above all a pension and savings group in Norway and Sweden, with its head office at Lysaker. The Swedish pension company SPP was bought from Handelsbanken in 2007, and in 2022 Danica Pensjon in Norway and the savings app Kron were added. Asset management includes the SKAGEN and Delphi funds, the private equity firm Cubera and Denmark's AIP, which manages infrastructure and renewable energy funds. Alongside this, the group runs non-life and personal insurance and a bank for retail customers. Folketrygdfondet, the Norwegian state's fund manager, is the largest shareholder.

For the dividend, the guaranteed pensions are the most important thing to understand. These are older pension contracts with an interest rate guarantee, a portfolio in run-off that shrinks as the obligations expire. While they remain, they tie up a lot of capital, and Storebrand plans to pay out what they release, mainly through buybacks of its own shares. Under the policy the ordinary dividend is to be kept at least at the same amount in kroner as the year before, as long as solvency is good enough, and at an even higher solvency the board intends to propose an extra dividend or buybacks. At its capital markets day in December 2025 the group set out an ambition for double-digit annual dividend growth up to and including 2028.

Tax for investors outside Norway

Storebrand is a Norwegian company, and Norwegian companies deduct 25% withholding tax on dividends to shareholders resident abroad. If a tax treaty between Norway and your country gives a lower rate, you can get it when the dividend is paid by giving your custodian or account operator the documentation, or apply to the Norwegian Tax Administration for a refund afterwards, within five years. Shareholders resident in the EEA may instead use the risk-free return allowance where that gives less tax, and companies resident in the EEA may be exempt. Your own country may also tax the dividend.
